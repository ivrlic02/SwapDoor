## 1. What is HCI?

HCI is the study of the physical, psychological, and theoretical aspects of how people interact with computers. It seeks to understand the extent to which computer systems successfully support user interaction versus causing user frustration.

HCI is framed as having **three sides**:
1. **The user** — could be a single person, a group, or a sequence/succession of users.
2. **The computer** — any technology, not just traditional desktop computers.
3. **The way they work together** — the interaction itself.

A supporting video reference was shown to motivate "Why HCI?": https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kKT_09pARN4

## 2. Why does HCI matter? ("Should we really care?")

The lecture argues that bad design has real, sometimes serious, consequences:
- You can crash your car and get injured (poor interface/control design in vehicles).
- A business can fail ("go out of business") because of unusable products.
- Poor usability causes frustration and mistakes, making tasks take longer than they should.
- You can lose an election due to bad interface/ballot design — cited example: **US Presidential Election 2000** (the "butterfly ballot" controversy in Florida is the implied real-world case, though not named explicitly in the slide text).
- You can lose a competition due to a presenter/interface error — cited example: **Miss Universe 2015** (referring to the infamous on-stage announcement error, though again not spelled out in the slide text itself).

Slide 5 also references numerous "badly designed things" encountered in everyday life, prompting the viewer to ask "Which side [is broken]?" and "Guess the default action" — a classic usability exercise. An example website referenced as a source of bad design examples: **http://www.baddesigns.com**

## 3. Historical/technological context: Moore's Law and the human bottleneck

The deck presents a conceptual, non-numeric diagram spanning roughly **1950 to 2050** (with one version stretching back to **2000 BC**) plotting:
- **Computer abilities** growing over time — driven by: transistors, speed, disc/storage capacity, and cost (cost decreasing, capability increasing) — this is **Moore's Law**.
- **Human abilities** — shown as comparatively flat/unchanging across the same timeline, since human cognitive and physical capabilities do not scale the way computing power does.

The key argument built from this comparison: as computer capability grows exponentially while human capability stays essentially constant, **the bottleneck in a computing system shifts from the machine to the human** — i.e., **system performance is increasingly limited by human factors, not hardware**. This is presented as a core motivation for HCI as a discipline: if humans are the bottleneck, then designing systems around human capabilities and limitations becomes the critical engineering problem.

## 5. The interface design process and usability engineering

The lecture lays out a pipeline/model for how interfaces get designed, structured as a progression through goals, methods, and products:

**Step 1 — Articulate:**
- Who the users are
- Their key tasks

**Step 2 — Brainstorm designs**

**Step 3 — Refined designs**

**Step 4 — Completed designs**

This progression is cross-referenced with three parallel tracks:

- **Goals:**
  - Task-centered system design
  - Participatory design
  - User-centered design

- **Methods (associated with each goal), including:**
  - Psychology of everyday things
  - User involvement
  - Representation & metaphors
  - Graphical screen design
  - Interface guidelines / style guides
  - Participatory interaction
  - Task scenario walk-throughs
  - Usability testing (evaluation)
  - Heuristic evaluation
  - Field testing
  - High-fidelity prototyping methods
  - Low-fidelity prototyping methods (including throw-away paper prototypes)

- **Products (outputs at each stage):**
  - User and task descriptions
  - Testable prototypes
  - Alpha/beta systems or complete specifications

**Why bother with a formal interface design process?**
- Large software projects regularly go over budget/cost.
- Managers cited **four usability-related reasons** for overruns:
  1. Users requested changes.
  2. Overlooked tasks.
  3. Users did not understand their own requirements.
  4. Insufficient user-developer communication and understanding.

**Core principle:** *Usability engineering is software engineering.* The lecture uses the aphorism **"Pay a little now, or pay a lot later!"** — meaning it's cheap to fix usability problems early in design and expensive to fix them after deployment.

**Risk of skipping this process:** it's far too easy to jump straight into detailed design that is:
- Founded on incorrect requirements,
- Has inappropriate dialogue/interaction flow,
- Is not easily used,
- Is never tested until it's too late.

## Key terms and concepts glossary (for quick reference / retrieval)

- **HCI (Human-Computer Interaction):** Discipline studying and designing the interaction between users and computing systems, spanning design, implementation, and evaluation.
- **Usability:** The quality of how easily and effectively a system supports users in accomplishing tasks without frustration or error.
- **Moore's Law:** The trend of exponential growth in computing capability (transistors, speed, storage) over time, contrasted in this lecture with static human capability to argue humans are the performance bottleneck.
- **Task-centered system design:** Designing around a clear understanding of user tasks.
- **User-centered design:** Designing with direct, ongoing user involvement.
- **Participatory design:** A design approach involving users as active participants/co-designers.
- **Heuristic evaluation:** An evaluation method using established usability principles/heuristics to find interface problems.
- **Low-fidelity prototyping:** Quick, low-cost mockups (e.g., paper prototypes) used early in design for cheap iteration.
- **High-fidelity prototyping:** More polished, realistic prototypes used later in design.
- **Design of everyday things:** Reference to the broader design philosophy (associated with Don Norman) concerning how everyday objects/interfaces should communicate their use to people.
